Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Aurora

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year in hard-water areas.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually encodes the month and year it was built, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that point and is also showing discolored water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement once the tank fails.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Common Water Heater Problems in Aurora

The majority of water heater problems we see in Aurora come back to what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Aging equipment is the other half of the story. A standard tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod is often used up, leaving the tank open to rust. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Homes on private wells may bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and seismic straps in areas that require them. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early.
